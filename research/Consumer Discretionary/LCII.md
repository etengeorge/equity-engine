**The case for the price.** There is no standalone consensus to steelman, because LCI Industries has agreed to be acquired. On 30 June 2026 LCII entered an Agreement and Plan of Merger with Patrick Industries under which each LCI share converts into 1.2440 Patrick shares, with legacy Patrick holders owning about 52% of the combined company and LCI holders about 48%. Closing is expected in the first half of 2027, subject to both shareholder votes and regulatory approval, with more than $150 million of run-rate cost synergies targeted within three years. At $93.40 the shares imply a Patrick price of about $75.08, so what the market is 'assuming' about LCI's cash flows is only its assumption about Patrick's, plus a deal-completion probability.

**What changed.** Everything the model does not know. (1) The merger: announced 2026-06-30, confirmed in the 8-K filed 2026-09-10 under Item 8.01 reporting an event dated 2026-09-04, which recites the Merger Agreement with Patrick Industries, Planet First Merger Sub Inc. and Planet Second Merger Sub LLC — a two-step structure with LCI surviving first as a Patrick subsidiary and then merging into the second sub. The cluster of Item 8.01/9.01 8-Ks on 2026-07-22, 2026-08-07, 2026-08-10 and 2026-09-10 with no 2.02 among them is the merger-communications pattern. (2) The business, which is in a genuine downturn: Q2 2026 adjusted net sales fell 4.5% to $1,057.5 million; the company LOWERED 2026 North American RV wholesale shipment guidance to 280,000-300,000 units from 315,000-330,000; July net sales were approximately $315 million, down 4%. Operating margin guidance of 7.5-8.0% was reaffirmed on self-help cost actions, and adjusted EPS rose 13% to $2.70 on a 4% sales decline. The company also paid off the remaining $92.0 million of its 2026 convertible notes at maturity in the first half.

**Devil's advocate.**
- Strongest counter: The strongest case for modelling LCII anyway is that an all-stock merger is not a cash-out: an LCI holder ends up owning 48% of a real operating business, so the underlying cash flows still matter and refusing to value them is intellectually lazy. And the deal could break — it needs two shareholder votes and an antitrust review of a combination of the two largest RV component suppliers, which is a real concentration question. If it breaks, LCI's standalone value is exactly what the engine is trying to compute.
- What would prove it: Antitrust clearance or a second request from the FTC; the S-4 becoming effective and both shareholder votes scheduled; and the deal spread. If the spread widens materially against 1.2440 times Patrick's price, the market is pricing break risk and standalone value becomes the live question again.
- Already visible today: Partly, and it cuts against modelling it. The board approvals were unanimous on both sides, the structure is a merger of near-equals (52/48) rather than a premium takeout, and LCII's 5-day and 21-day moves of -9.0% and -12.0% are ordinary dispersion — which is exactly the BKH signature in LESSONS.md: in a share exchange the price floats with the ACQUIRER, so the tape looks normal and neither the SLAB pinned-price tell nor the APGE items 3.01/5.01 tell fires. What I could not check is the current Patrick share price against the 1.2440 ratio, so I cannot quantify the spread and therefore cannot quantify the implied break probability.
- Left unresolved: The antitrust path. A combination of LCI and Patrick is a combination of the two dominant suppliers of RV components to the same Elkhart OEM customer base, and I did not find any disclosure of the HSR status. That is the one thing that would return this name to a standalone valuation question, and I could not resolve it.

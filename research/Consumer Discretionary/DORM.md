**The case for the price.** Dorman is a motor-vehicle aftermarket parts supplier with a structural tailwind — a US fleet of record average age — and a self-help margin story that has worked. At $121.67 the market pays roughly 14.2x the midpoint of 2026 guided adjusted EPS of $8.50-8.80, or roughly 18-19x the ex-tariff-recovery earning power underneath it. The informed case for that price is specific: gross margin has structurally risen from the low thirties to an underlying ~41% through complexity-based pricing and the 'Dorman 2.0' programme; the company navigated the 2025-26 tariff shock, paid the cost, and is now recovering it while simultaneously taking 'targeted pricing actions as a result of a more stable tariff environment', which is a demonstration of pricing power rather than a windfall; the supplier network is diversified away from single-country concentration; and the share count is shrinking, with $47 million repurchased in Q2 alone against a $3.6 billion capitalisation. Paying 18x for a 3-5% top-line grower with demonstrated pricing power, a defensive end market and a buyback is unremarkable. Note too that the market has NOT been complacent: the stock is -14.9% over 252 days and -12.2% over 63 days in a year of record reported EPS, which is the tape already discounting the recovery as non-recurring.

**What changed.** The 2026 numbers are inflated by a one-time tariff recovery, and the company says so in its own headline. Q2 2026 (filed 2026-08-03): net sales $544.6 million, up just 0.7%; diluted EPS $2.93, up 53%; adjusted diluted EPS $3.08, up 50%; $152.6 million of operating cash flow in a single quarter — and the bullet above it reads 'Earnings and cash from operating activities benefited from IEEPA tariff cost recovery'. Gross margin was 46.1% against 40.6%, a 550 basis point jump on 0.7% sales growth, which is the documented IEEPA signature: gross margin RISES while the underlying line is flat. Full-year guidance updated to net sales growth of 3-5%, diluted EPS $7.93-8.23 and adjusted $8.50-8.80 — which implies H2 diluted EPS of $3.54-3.84 against H1's $4.39, i.e. the company is guiding the second half DOWN from the first. The mirror image is in the FY2025 10-K, which explains the trough directly: operating cash flow fell $117.4 million 'primarily as a result of cash used to fund investments in inventory to meet customer demand and to pay for increased tariffs on imports'. H1 2026 then reversed it — operating cash flow $196.4 million against $59.8 million, with inventories a $150.5 million SOURCE of cash against an $89.7 million use a year earlier. Also: $450 million of senior notes issued in H1 2026, refinancing the prior term loan.

**Base case.** I override the base before touching the growth rate, because the engine's base is a tariff artifact in both directions. Its series is FY2025 $75.7M, FY2024 $191.6M, FY2023 $164.8M — I tied all three to the filed cash flow statements — and the 0.46x collapse in the newest year is tariff cash paid plus an inventory build, not a decline in earning power. The three-year mean of $144M plus a $36M after-tax interest add-back gives the brief's $180.1M, which embeds that one-off. Two independent routes put normalised FCFF near $230 million. Route one: FY2024, the cleanest year in the window, produced $191.6M of free cash flow; adding the same $36M interest add-back gives $228M. Route two, built from the income statement: normalised revenue of about $2.15 billion at a normalised ~15.5% operating margin — the margin implied by Q2 2025's $2.06 adjusted EPS before any recovery, not the 46.1%-gross-margin version — gives about $335M of EBIT, $251M after tax, plus $56M of D&A, less $40M of capex and about $35M of working-capital growth, which lands at $232M. I use $230 million. Against that base the market requires +7.9% five-year FCFF growth, not the +14.5% the brief prints. I underwrite +5%: the company guides 3-5% revenue growth, and 5% enterprise FCFF growth needs roughly 0.3 percentage points a year of further operating-margin expansion on top of an already-elevated base. I deliberately do NOT credit the buyback, because the reverse DCF is an enterprise-level calculation and repurchases do not create FCFF growth — a distinction that matters a lot on this name, where the per-share story is better than the enterprise story.

**Devil's advocate.**
- Strongest counter: The strongest counter is that my base override is the entire verdict and I chose it. At $230 million the market requires +7.9% and I underwrite +5%, giving -14.3%; but push the base up 15% to $264 million — well inside the error of an estimate assembled from a normalised margin I inferred rather than read — and the same +5% growth gives -0.2%, which is fair. So the whole call is a rounding decision on a number no filing states. Worse, my normalisation may be too harsh: if the tariff cost recovery is genuinely a recovery of cash Dorman already spent, then BOTH 2025 and the first half of 2026 are distorted, and the true normalised margin sits above Q2 2025's depressed 40.6% gross margin, which would push the base toward the upper end and the verdict to fair or better.
- What would prove it: A clean post-recovery quarter: gross margin and operating cash flow in a period with no IEEPA recovery in it, which should be Q4 2026 or Q1 2027, read against the 3-5% revenue guide.
- Already visible today: Partly, and it is why I am not calling this rich. The company's own guidance implies H2 2026 diluted EPS of $3.54-3.84 against H1's $4.39 — management is telling the market the second half steps down — but that still contains some recovery, so it is not yet the clean quarter. And the tape has already moved: -14.9% over twelve months on record reported earnings says the market is not paying for the recovery. When the market has already discounted the thing I am worried about, a further -14% estimate of mine is not an edge.
- Left unresolved: I could not size the IEEPA recovery in dollars. Neither the Q2 release nor the guidance quantifies it, so my normalised margin is inferred from the year-ago comparison rather than read off a disclosure, and the entire base — and therefore the entire gap — rests on that inference. That is the reason this is no_edge with a number rather than a rich call.

**Key risks.** The IEEPA recovery laps in 2027 against a 2026 comparison that contains it; Customer concentration: three retailers with structural pricing power over the supplier; A re-escalation of tariffs, or a second recovery cycle, either of which makes two more years of reported numbers uninterpretable; My FCFF base is an estimate, not a disclosure — the gap is inside its error bar
**Watch for.** The first quarter with no IEEPA recovery in it: gross margin against the underlying ~41%; Any quantification of the tariff recovery in the 2026 10-K, which would convert my inferred base into a read one; 2027 guidance — whether management guides growth off the recovery-inflated 2026 base or resets it

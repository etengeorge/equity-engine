**The case for the price.** Balchem is a genuine compounder and the market is paying for duration, not for the next five years. It holds a dominant position in choline and encapsulation chemistry, sells into human nutrition, animal nutrition and specialty markets with real pricing power, earns high returns on capital, and has a long history of bolt-on acquisitions that it integrates well. Q2 2026 was a record quarter on every line — record net sales of $284.0M, record net earnings of $44.6M (+16.6% year over year), record adjusted EBITDA of $77.9M — with growth in all three reporting segments and Human Nutrition and Health up 10.0% to $176.9M with record segment earnings of $42.4M. Earnings per share are compounding in the mid-teens. A 2.5% FCFF yield on a business like that is not a forecast that free cash flow grows 19% for five years; it is a statement that the terminal value is long and the cash flows are exceptionally safe, and a two-stage model with terminal growth pinned at 2.0% will always call such a business expensive.

**What changed.** Q2 2026 (2026-07-31): record net sales $284.0M; GAAP net earnings $44.6M, up 16.6% year over year; diluted EPS $1.39 against $1.17 (six months $2.63 against $2.30); record adjusted EBITDA $77.9M; cash from operations $46.7M with free cash flow of $36.2M. Human Nutrition and Health record sales of $176.9M, up $16.1M or 10.0%, with record segment earnings from operations of $42.4M, up 10.5%, partly offset by higher manufacturing costs. Management describes healthy growth in all three segments. A 2026-07-27 8-K carries items 1.01 and 2.03 — a material agreement and a new debt obligation — which on the timing looks like a credit facility refinancing rather than an acquisition, though I did not read the agreement itself. No company-specific news in the store for the last 90 days.

**Base case.** The question is not whether Balchem is a good business — it plainly is — but what five-year free cash flow growth the price requires and whether the company has ever produced it. The engine's own series answers that: FCFF of $167.5M, $145.9M and $151.7M, newest first, which is about 5% a year of growth over two years. Earnings are compounding faster than cash flow, in the mid-teens, and the gap is capex and working capital as Balchem builds capacity. My 8% splits the difference and is deliberately generous relative to the cash record: it assumes high-single-digit organic revenue growth led by Human Nutrition at its current 10%, modest margin expansion as the new capacity fills, and continued bolt-on M&A funded internally. What it does not assume is the +19.1% the price requires at the engine's 7.2% WACC — and the honest hurdle is higher still, because stock compensation is 11% of the FCFF base and reported operating cash flow adds it back as though it were free. It is not free; it is a real transfer to employees. Expensing it takes the FCFF base to $121.1M and the required growth to +22.5%. I am explicitly underwriting the stock-compensation-expensed version, so the number my 8% has to beat is 22.5%, not 19.1%.

**Devil's advocate.**
- Strongest counter: That calling a high-quality compounder 'rich' on a two-stage DCF is the single most reliably wrong trade of the last fifteen years, and that the model's structure — not Balchem's price — is what produces the answer. Terminal growth is pinned at 2.0%, which is a choice, not a fact, and it is the wrong choice for a business that can plausibly grow at nominal GDP plus a couple of points indefinitely with pricing power and a 60%-plus share in choline. Raise terminal growth to 3% and the required five-year growth falls materially, because most of the value in a 2.5%-yield asset sits in the terminal. On top of that the model discounts at only 7.2% — so it is simultaneously being generous on the discount rate and harsh on the terminal, and I should be suspicious of a verdict that depends on which of those two errors is larger.
- What would prove it: Whether Balchem's free cash flow can actually compound in the high teens for a sustained period, or whether it stays in the mid-single digits while earnings run ahead of it. The specific observable is the multi-year FCFF series against the EPS series.
- Already visible today: Yes, and it is why the counter-argument does not flip the verdict. Free cash flow of $167.5M / $145.9M / $151.7M is roughly 5% growth while diluted EPS went from $1.17 to $1.39 in the quarter — the divergence between earnings and cash is visible and persistent, and it is the cash flow the DCF discounts. Even granting the terminal-growth objection in full, the gap between a demonstrated ~5%, my generous 8%, and a required 22.5% is not a modelling quibble; it is a factor of three. The beta point also runs my way rather than against me for once: beta is 0.45 on an R-squared of 0.161, and the standing lesson is that a poorly fitted regression attenuates beta toward zero and understates WACC. Correcting Balchem's beta upward would RAISE the required growth above 22.5%, making the name look richer still, not cheaper. That is the rare case where the known engine bias reinforces the conclusion instead of manufacturing it.
- Left unresolved: I did not read the 2026-07-27 item-1.01/2.03 agreement, so I cannot rule out that it is an acquisition rather than a refinancing — if it were a large acquisition it would change both enterprise value and the forward cash flow base. Given the 3% debt weight in the model I think that is unlikely, but I did not verify it. I also cannot resolve the terminal-growth objection, because it is a question about the model's constants rather than about Balchem, and this engine gives me no way to override them.

**Key risks.** The valuation is almost entirely terminal value at a 2.5% FCFF yield, so it is highly sensitive to the terminal growth and discount rate constants rather than to Balchem's operations; Free cash flow has grown at roughly 5% a year while earnings compound in the mid-teens — capex and working capital are absorbing the difference; Stock compensation is 11% of the FCFF base and is added back as free cash flow; expensing it raises the required growth from 19.1% to 22.5%; Agricultural and animal-nutrition cyclicality in two of the three segments
**Watch for.** Free cash flow conversion: whether FCF starts tracking earnings growth as the capacity investment cycle completes; Human Nutrition and Health growth holding at or above 10% for another two or three quarters; Whether the 2026-07-27 material agreement turns out to be an acquisition, which would reset the cash flow base; Any sign that higher manufacturing costs are becoming a persistent margin drag rather than a quarterly offset

**Data quality.** No flags were raised and I found none that should have been. The FCFF series ($167.5M / $145.9M / $151.7M) is stable and non-lumpy, the balance sheet is current, share count is not stale, and I found no completed acquisition or equity issuance inside the window that would invalidate enterprise value. Verified against the Q2 release: net sales $284.0M, net earnings $44.6M, adjusted EBITDA $77.9M, cash from operations $46.7M, free cash flow $36.2M — the $36.2M quarterly figure annualises to roughly $145M and is consistent with the engine's $138.0M FCFF base, so the base is a fair representation of current cash generation rather than a peak or a trough. Two items are open. First, I did not read the 2026-07-27 item-1.01/2.03 agreement and so cannot confirm it is a refinancing rather than an acquisition. Second, beta of 0.45 sits on an R-squared of 0.161, below the ~0.15-0.20 threshold where the standing low-R-squared bias becomes unreliable — but here the bias understates the discount rate and therefore understates how demanding the price is, so correcting it would strengthen this verdict rather than weaken it.

The case for the price. NMI is a monoline private mortgage insurer trading at 1.12x reported tangible book ($40.10 against a 2026-06-30 book value per share of $35.89, or $36.88 excluding net unrealized investment losses) while earning a 15.9% annualized ROE. The market's answer is not an opinion about NMIH's accounting, it is a view on the shape of the return distribution: mortgage insurance earns 14-18% for a decade and then loses a large fraction of book value in one housing credit cycle. In 2007-2011 PMI Group and Triad Guaranty failed outright and MGIC and Radian each lost roughly their entire book value and diluted heavily to survive. NMIH was founded in 2012, so its entire portfolio and its entire reported return history sit inside the post-crisis recovery and have never been tested by a downturn. A price near tangible book on a 15% reported ROE is what a bimodal return distribution looks like when the market prices the left tail, and the market applies the same answer to the whole cohort: Radian 0.93x, Essent 1.00x, Enact 1.17x, NMIH 1.17x on the engine's book. Four independent boards, four sets of analysts, one answer.

What changed. Q2 2026 (reported 2026-07-30, 10-Q filed 2026-07-31): net income $105.8M / $1.38 diluted, adjusted $106.0M. ROE 15.9% in Q2 against 15.2% in Q1 and 16.2% in Q2 2025; H1 ROE 15.5% against 17.1% a year earlier, i.e. the return is trending DOWN. Book value per share $35.89, ex-unrealized $36.88 (up 15% from $32.08 a year earlier). Shareholders' equity $2.7bn. $31.4M of stock repurchased in the quarter. Credit is deteriorating slowly at the margin: 8,020 loans in default at 2026-06-30 on a 1.16% default rate against 7,661 and 1.12% at 2025-12-31, and the default count has risen for five periods (6,709 -> 7,093 -> 7,661 -> 8,044 -> 8,020). The 10-Q states claims incurred on CURRENT year defaults increased 'due to an increase in the total number of new delinquencies emerging during the period tied to the growth and natural seasoning of our portfolio'. Against that, earnings carry $40.6M of FAVOURABLE PRIOR YEAR DEVELOPMENT in H1 2026 ($43.8M in H1 2025) 'primarily due to cure activity', with $122.6M of gross reserves on prior-year defaults remaining. No 8-K under items 1.01, 2.01, 3.02 or 4.02 in the last two quarters; the filing list is routine (2.02 earnings, 5.07 annual meeting).

Base case. The engine's 16.0% 'sustainable ROTCE' reproduces exactly as the mean of the three fiscal-year ratios [15.00%, 16.24%, 16.72%] (net income $388.9M/$360.1M/$322.1M over equity $2,592.0M/$2,217.4M/$1,926.0M). That series is MONOTONICALLY DECLINING, so per the VLY rule the mean is describing a company that no longer exists: the newest observation is 15.00% and the current reported figure is 15.5% for H1. More importantly, the AMSF reserve-release detector fires. H1 2026 net income of $205.1M includes $40.6M pre-tax of favourable prior-year development, about $32.1M after tax at the 21% statutory rate, so ex-development H1 net income is roughly $173.0M and the ex-development ROE is about 13.1% rather than 15.5%. That is the honest current-accident-year return, and it is the number to price, because the releases are a drawdown of a $122.6M reserve stock that is finite while new delinquencies are rising. I do not supply a rotce_override. The reason is the one the GLRE, HAE, CLVT and WLFC entries in LESSONS.md all reach: the input that decides the sign here is the COST OF EQUITY, and the schema has no field for it. The engine's 7.98% comes from a beta of 0.53 at an R-squared of 0.155 measured over 104 weeks of a benign credit environment - which is precisely the period in which a mortgage insurer's beta is lowest and its tail risk least visible. In the Gordon form the model uses, justified P/TBV = (ROTCE - g)/(CoE - g) with g fixed at 2%, that puts 6.0 percentage points in the denominator, so the constant does more work than the business. Supplying a 13.1% ROTCE would price it through the 7.98% cost of equity and print a fair value of roughly $64 a share, +60%, which I do not believe. The verdict is the output; no number is recorded.

Devil's advocate.

  • Strongest counter: That the cohort evidence I am leaning on is not evidence of a framework error but of a genuine, persistent sector mispricing, and that the post-crisis mortgage-insurance business is structurally different enough that the 2008 analogy is doing illegitimate work. The specifics are strong: NMIH has no legacy book at all, PMIERs imposes a capital standard that did not exist in 2007, the GSEs' loan-level pricing and the QM/ATR rules make the vintages that killed the legacy insurers impossible to write today, and a large share of the risk is ceded through ILNs and quota-share. Tangible book per share compounded 15% in twelve months ($32.08 to $36.88 ex-unrealized) while the company bought back stock. A business compounding book at 15% and trading at 1.09x ex-unrealized book does not need a re-rating to deliver a good return.
  • What would prove it: The current accident-year loss ratio, stripped of prior-year development, through a period of rising delinquency - and whether cure rates hold as the 2024-2026 vintages season. Concretely: four to six more quarters of the default count and the current-year claims line, and whether the $122.6M prior-year reserve stock keeps releasing. If the ex-development return holds near 13-14% while the default rate climbs through 1.3-1.5%, the structural argument is right and 1.1x book is too cheap.
  • Already visible today: Partly, and it cuts both ways. For the bull: book value per share is genuinely compounding at 15% and the company is buying stock, which is not the behaviour of a management team seeing credit turn. For the bear: the default count has risen for five consecutive periods, the default rate has gone 1.12% to 1.16%, the 10-Q attributes rising current-year claims to new delinquencies emerging, the H1 ROE fell from 17.1% to 15.5% year over year, and $40.6M of the H1 result is a release from a finite reserve stock. Neither side is settled and the decisive observable - a loss ratio through an actual downturn - does not exist yet, by construction.
  • Left unresolved: The cost of equity, which is the whole verdict and which I cannot put in the record. The sign of the gap flips between an 11.9% cost of equity (at the 13.1% ex-development ROTCE) and a 14.5% one (at the engine's 16.0%); the engine uses 7.98%. I cannot derive the right figure for a monoline whose loss distribution is bimodal from any free source, because the relevant observation - the beta of a mortgage insurer through a housing downturn - requires a downturn. I also cannot distinguish 'the market prices a tail the model cannot see' from 'the market is wrong about this cohort' on the evidence available; I have asserted the former because four independent names agree and because the mechanism is specific and historically documented, but that is a judgement, not a measurement.

The case for the price. Archrock is the largest US contract natural-gas compression owner, renting horsepower to producers and midstream operators on multi-year contracts. At $32.96 it carries a $5.8B market capitalisation and roughly $8.1B of enterprise value against guided 2026 adjusted EBITDA of $865-885M — about 9.3x. The bull case is a structurally tight compression market, high utilisation, a healthy order book and durable natural-gas demand; the price already embeds it. The case for the price rather than above it: adjusted EBITDA in Q2 2026 was $212.6M against $212.7M a year earlier, essentially FLAT, and the company tightened full-year guidance by cutting the top end from $915M to $885M on make-ready costs, lube oil, weaker aftermarket demand and higher incentive compensation. Archrock has committed to $250-275M of growth capital this year and $1.4-1.6B cumulatively across 2027-2030, so this is a capital-hungry business whose current earnings are flat while it spends; a 9.3x multiple is a normal, not a generous, price for that.

What changed. Nothing that invalidates the model, but the Q2 release (2026-08-05) supplies the numbers that dismantle the engine's reading. Adjusted EBITDA flat year over year at $212.6M; full-year 2026 guidance TIGHTENED to $865-885M from $865-915M; growth capital expenditures reaffirmed at $250-275M and maintenance capital expenditures guided at $125-135M separately; multi-year growth capex guidance of $1.4-1.6B introduced for 2027-2030. Quarterly depreciation and amortisation is $71.5M, about $286M annualised. The NGCS acquisition closed in Q2 2025 for $296.6M of cash net of cash acquired — an acquisition INSIDE the three-year cash-flow window, which is part of why the FCF series is as lumpy as it is. The 6.250% senior notes due 2028 were redeemed in full on 2026-04-01. No company-specific news is in the store for the last 90 days, which lowers my confidence that nothing else happened rather than confirming it; the sector feed is all oil-price and OPEC+ macro, with Brent above $100.

Base case. The engine's $150.9M FCFF base is CFO less TOTAL capital expenditure, and total capex here is $375-410M of which only $125-135M is maintenance. So the base charges Archrock the full cost of expanding its fleet and then the reverse DCF applies a growth rate to what is left — the same dollar counted twice, once as a cost and once as the growth it buys. This is the exact mirror of the CTOS, ALTG and WLFC rental-capex bug: there growth capex was MISSING from capex and manufactured false cheapness; here it is fully captured and misread as sustaining, manufacturing false richness. Corrected, the base is the cash the EXISTING fleet throws off: EBITDA of about $875M less maintenance capex of $130M, taxed — roughly $600M of sustaining FCFF, four times the engine's number. The consistent pairing for that base is ZERO growth, because I have removed the investment that would produce growth. On $600M and 0% the market implies -1.3%, and the fair value lands at about $34.50 against a $32.96 price. Two independent methods therefore agree that Archrock is close to fairly valued: a sustaining-cash-flow DCF at roughly no growth, and a 9.3x EV/EBITDA multiple that sits squarely in the range where contract compression peers trade.

Devil's advocate.

  • Strongest counter: My correction may go too far in the other direction. Maintenance capital expenditure of $125-135M is a management-defined non-GAAP number and it is less than HALF of $286M of depreciation and amortisation on a fleet of rotating equipment that genuinely wears out. If true economic sustaining capex is nearer D&A, the base falls from about $600M to about $435M, the market's implied growth rises from -1.3% to +6.7%, and Archrock stops looking fair and starts looking demanding — because a company whose adjusted EBITDA was FLAT year over year has to find that 6.7% somewhere. The Q2 print is the bear's best evidence: flat EBITDA in a market management calls 'structurally tight', while spending $250-275M a year.
  • What would prove it: Maintenance capital expenditure as a share of depreciation over several years, and whether the compression fleet's average age rises. More directly: 2027 adjusted EBITDA against 2026's $865-885M, which is the return on the growth capital already spent. If $250-275M a year buys no EBITDA growth, the growth programme is not earning its cost of capital and the sustaining base with zero growth is the generous reading, not the conservative one.

The case for the price. At $6.34 the market is pricing Teladoc as a melting ice cube. Revenue has rolled over from a 2023 peak of $2.60B to $2.53B, BetterHelp is shrinking, the company has posted three straight years of GAAP losses including a $1.0B impairment year, and a $1.0B convertible matures in 2027 against $781M of cash. The bear owning this price believes direct-to-consumer telehealth has no moat, that BetterHelp's customer acquisition cost rises as fast as its pricing, and that Integrated Care's enterprise contracts reprice down at renewal. Paying roughly 7x FCFF-after-stock-comp for a business with no demonstrated organic growth and a refinancing to clear is not obviously wrong.

Base case. The engine's fundamentals extract records total_debt of $42.4M for Teladoc. The actual figure is $996.7M of convertible senior notes plus that $42.4M. The cause is a concept-alias gap: edgar.py's total_debt alias list covers LongTermDebtNoncurrent, LongTermDebt, LongTermDebtAndCapitalLeaseObligations, DebtLongtermAndShorttermCombinedAmount, Borrowings and NoncurrentPortionOfNoncurrentBorrowings, and debt_current covers DebtCurrent and LongTermDebtCurrent — none of which capture the ConvertibleNotesPayable / ConvertibleDebt family that a convertible issuer tags. The consequence is large and directional: the model computes EV of $413M where the correct figure is roughly $1.41B ($1.152B market cap + $996.7M notes + $42.4M other debt − $781M cash). Recomputing the reverse DCF on the corrected EV at the model's own 13.28% WACC moves the growth the market implies from −43.0% to −15.8% on the reported FCFF base, and from −30.1% to −2.0% once stock compensation is expensed. In other words the entire +220% screen gap is an artifact of missing debt. On a corrected EV, a business whose revenue is declining roughly 1.5% a year being priced for about −2% FCFF growth is approximately fairly valued, and there is no edge here at all.

Devil's advocate.

  • Strongest counter: The strongest case against my no_model call is that the convertible is a near-equity instrument and I am double-counting it. If the 2027 notes convert rather than being repaid in cash, they never consume the $996.7M and the correct EV is closer to the model's figure, with the dilution showing up in share count instead. There is also a case that even on the corrected EV the stock is cheap: at $1.41B EV against $205M of FCFF with stock comp expensed at the latest run rate of $80.4M, the market is asking for roughly −5% perpetual decline, and Integrated Care is not actually declining — it is BetterHelp that is shrinking. A sum-of-parts owner would argue Integrated Care alone is worth more than the whole EV.
  • What would prove it: The conversion question is settled by the stock price against the conversion price: the 2027 Notes were struck in May 2020 when Teladoc traded near its peak, so the conversion price sits far above $6.34 and the notes are deep out of the money. That means they will be repaid or refinanced in cash, not converted, and they belong in enterprise value in full. On the sum-of-parts point, the settling evidence would be segment-level operating cash flow disclosure separating Integrated Care from BetterHelp.
  • Already visible today: Yes, and it defeats the counter. The notes being classified as CURRENT on the 30 June 2026 balance sheet is itself the tell — an issuer reclassifies a convertible to current when it expects to settle it, and a deeply out-of-the-money convertible maturing in 2027 against $781M of cash is a cash refinancing obligation, not equity. So the debt belongs in EV and the model is wrong. What is NOT visible is the segment cash-flow split, so I cannot adjudicate the sum-of-parts argument.

That is a question for a rerun after the alias list is fixed, not something to guess at today.

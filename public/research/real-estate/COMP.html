The case for the price. At $10.56 and an $8.0B market cap, Compass is being priced as the consolidator of US residential brokerage. Having closed the Anywhere Real Estate merger on 9 January 2026, it now owns Coldwell Banker, Century 21, Sotheby's International Realty and Corcoran alongside the Compass platform, and the combined entity transacts a very large share of US residential volume. The bull case is operating leverage on a fixed technology and brand cost base plus merger synergies against a housing market that has been transaction-depressed for three years by mortgage rates — so volumes are near a cyclical floor and any rate relief flows straight to a business whose costs are largely agent commissions that scale with revenue. Compass has also turned cash generative: operating cash flow of $216.7M in 2025 against negative $25.9M two years earlier.

What changed. The Anywhere Real Estate merger closed on 9 January 2026, which is after the 31 December 2025 balance sheet the engine's extract is built on. The 2026-08-06 10-Q confirms it directly, disclosing H1 2026 operating cash flow of $34 million driven by $114 million of net income adjusted for $316 million of depreciation and amortisation and $149 million of stock-based compensation, offset by a $401 million deferred income tax benefit and a $142 million working-capital outflow that included cash payments for Anywhere merger transaction and integration expenses and payments on liabilities assumed in the merger. Note the shape of that: reported net income is positive but is carried by a deferred tax benefit and enormous non-cash amortisation, while actual operating cash generation fell to $34 million in H1 from $96 million a year earlier. There is also a 2026-04-17 8-K under item 1.01 (a material agreement) which I did not read.

Base case. Compass is not a REIT — it is an asset-light brokerage whose economics are commission revenue against agent splits and platform cost, and there is nothing about its accounting that makes free cash flow to the firm meaningless the way it is for a REIT or a bank. On the face of it, it is closer to an operating company than several names the engine happily models. So the sector rule reaches the right answer here by the wrong route. Any enterprise value computed from those inputs would be badly wrong, and the merger sits inside what would be the cash-flow window, making the history non-comparable. Both the configured reason and the situational reason point to no_model, so that is what I record.

The case for the price. At $54.51 and a $7.3B market cap, Fluor is not being valued on trailing free cash flow by anyone — the trailing series is negative. Owners are paying for a de-risked engineering and construction franchise: a reimbursable-heavy backlog that removes the fixed-price losses that nearly destroyed the company last decade, exposure to data-centre, semiconductor-fab, nuclear and energy-transition capex, and a balance sheet holding roughly $3.0 billion of cash and marketable securities against $1.07 billion of debt after monetizing the entire NuScale stake for about $2.43 billion on a $570 million cost basis. The buyer is underwriting backlog conversion and a mid-single-digit segment margin on a large revenue base, plus roughly $1.9 billion of net cash to deploy. None of that is visible in a three-year FCFF mean.

What changed. Two things, both after the last 10-K and both decisive. First, Fluor completed the monetization of its NuScale stake: approximately $1.35 billion for 71 million shares in February 2026, $162.9 million in April, and $473 million for the final 40 million shares completed 23 April 2026, taking cumulative open-market proceeds to about $2.43 billion against $570 million invested. The 2026-08-07 10-Q shows $1,831 million of NuScale sale proceeds in investing cash flow for the six months. Second, and the reason the cash flow statement looks alarming: H1 2026 operating cash flow was NEGATIVE $207 million, and the filing's own footnote explains that it includes $357 million of federal and state tax payments primarily related to the 2025 conversion of the NuScale shares. Ex-tax, H1 operating cash flow was roughly positive $150 million. Cash and marketable securities were $3.0 billion at 30 June 2026 against $2.2 billion at 31 December 2025, with total debt of $1,072 million.

Base case. I am not issuing a growth rate because there is no defensible FCFF base to grow. The model's base of $157.2 million is the mean of negative $437.0 million, positive $664.0 million and positive $106.0 million — a spread the engine itself flags at 9.9x of the mean, containing a negative year. A mean of three numbers that disagree about their own sign is not a normalized cash flow, it is an average of noise, and compounding it for five years and capitalising a terminal value on it produces a fair value with no information in it. The distortion has an identifiable source rather than being random: Fluor's operating cash flow in this window is dominated by project working-capital swings and, in the most recent periods, by the tax consequences of a $2.43 billion equity monetization that is itself a non-recurring investing event. Engineering and construction firms are valued on backlog conversion and segment margin against enterprise value, not on a three-year mean of free cash flow to the firm, and this engine has no free data path to that. Compounding the problem, the enterprise value is stale: the model's $6.2 billion is built on the December 2025 balance sheet and misses roughly $0.8 billion of the cash build, so the true EV is nearer $5.4 billion. Correcting only that would make the name look cheaper without making the base any more meaningful. The honest answer is that the cash flows will not support a valuation here.

Devil's advocate.

  • Strongest counter: The counter to a no_model call is that I am hiding behind the data. The screen says Fluor requires +34.6% FCFF growth to justify its price — +42.4% with stock compensation expensed — and a −62.8% gap against the naive baseline. Even granting that the base is noisy, a business that has just posted a negative operating cash flow year and needs a 35-42% compound growth rate to justify its market price is expensive on almost any reading, and 'no_model' lets an overvalued stock off the hook. A disciplined engine should be willing to say 'rich' when the required growth is absurd, rather than retreating to a technicality.
  • What would prove it: Whether the required growth is genuinely absurd or an artifact of the base. If normalized FCFF is truly near $157 million, the +34.6% requirement stands and the name is rich. If normalized FCFF is closer to the ex-tax H1 run rate, the required growth falls sharply and the 'rich' call collapses.
  • Already visible today: Yes, and it defeats the counter. H1 2026 operating cash flow ex the $357 million NuScale tax payment was roughly positive $150 million, which annualises well above the $157 million full-year FCFF base the model uses — before subtracting capex, but also before the seasonally stronger second half that E&C working capital typically delivers. So the base is not merely noisy, it is biased low by a one-time tax outflow on a gain that never passed through operating cash flow in the first place. Pair that with an enterprise value overstated by roughly $0.8 billion of unrecognised cash and the −62.8% 'rich' reading is not something I can stand behind. I therefore decline both the cheap and the rich call.
  • Left unresolved: I could not construct a normalized FCFF for Fluor from free sources with any confidence, because I would need to separate recurring project working-capital movement from the NuScale tax distortion across several years, and the 10-Q only gives me the current-period footnote. I also did not read the backlog and new-award figures closely enough to underwrite conversion, which is the actual driver of value here.

Key risks. Free cash flow to the firm is not a meaningful metric for this business over the available window — the three-year series spans a 9.9x range and includes a negative year; Fixed-price contract risk is the historical failure mode for Fluor and does not show up in a cash-flow mean until it has already happened; The NuScale windfall is spent capital now; the remaining business must be judged on backlog margin, and roughly $1.9B of net cash invites capital-allocation risk; Stock compensation is 33% of the model's FCFF base, so even the base that exists overstates owner earnings Watch for.
